After no. 20 the sequence is interrupted for four pages (pp. 22-25) by a chart of the Prophet's names. No. 20's transliteration and translation appear on p. 26, after that chart.
- **What the forty are:** nos. 1-25 are durud proper, mostly forms of the Ibrahimi durud. **Nos. 26-39 are forms of the tashahhud** (at-tahiyyat), and no. 40 is the one-line "Bismillahi was-salamu 'ala Rasulillah". Several items are not durud in the narrow sense: no. 33 contains no shahadah, nos. 35-37 have no salat on the Prophet beyond the salam, and so on. They are recorded here exactly as the PDF numbers them.

## Introduction (pp. 1-2): summary

The introduction argues that, after Allah, the Prophet ﷺ is the one who has benefited mankind most. He spent his nights and days praying for his ummah's salvation and endured persecution for it, and we owe him a debt we have forgotten. It quotes al-Ahzab 33:56 (Arabic, with an English rendering) and an Arabic couplet, ما مدحت محمدا بمقالتي ولكن مدحت مقالتي بمحمد, with an English verse paraphrase. It then notes that sending salutations is not only permitted but rewarded. It names two rewards: for each salutation Allah gives the reciter ten rewards, and the Prophet's intercession is granted to those who send salah and salam in abundance.

**Instructions on how and when to read:** the only instruction in the PDF is this sentence (p. 2), quoted verbatim:

> "In light of the phenomenal rewards available for the recitation of *Salaah* and *Salaam* one should endeavour to make it part of one's routine daily not just for one's journey of *ziyarah*."

The PDF says nothing else about when or how to read the forty. It does not say whether to read them in one sitting, on Friday, or a set number of times, and it gives no repeat counts.

## Notes on this transcription

- **Arabic** is copied from the page images, rendered from the PDF at about 7x zoom and checked line by line. Spelling and vowelling are kept **exactly as printed, including inconsistencies** (for example, اَللّٰهُمَّ versus أَللّٰهُمَّ, and اِبْرَاهِيْمَ versus أِبْرَاهِيْمَ). Nothing has been corrected or completed. The PDF writes "Al" (family) as alif with a hamza sign and a small dagger alif, encoded here as **أٰل**. It writes "Allah" with the Indo-Pak superscript alif, encoded as **اللّٰه**.
- **Pause marks:** the PDF's small ط and ج are encoded as the Unicode Quranic marks **ؕ** (U+0615, small high tah) and **ۚ** (U+06DA, small high jeem). Marks are given only where the PDF prints them.
- **Translation:** the PDF **does** print an English translation for every durud (italic, under the transliteration). **It is not reproduced verbatim here** because it is a copyrighted rendering and copying all forty would reproduce most of the booklet. For each durud this document gives the PDF page where its translation can be read, and a fresh translation of my own labelled "draft, for review". Where the PDF's English differs in substance from its own Arabic, that is pointed out in the notes.
- **Transliteration:** the PDF also prints its own transliteration (upper case, academic diacritics). The transliteration below is a fresh draft in plain popular style, as requested: ' for both 'ayn and hamza, no diacritics, assimilated article (ar-Rahman, as-salamu), case endings kept inside a phrase and dropped at a pause.
- **Repeat counts:** none are given anywhere in the PDF.
